## 1. The Linguistic Theory in Plain English

### The "Principle of Least Effort" (Why Native Speakers Drop Consonants)
In English linguistics, **Elision** means disappearing sounds.
- Why does a British person say *las(t) night* as */lɑːs-naɪt/* instead of */lɑːst naɪt/*?
- It is not laziness; it is **articulatory economy**.
- The sounds **/t/** and **/d/** are "alveolar plosives" (made by pressing the tip of the tongue against the ridge behind your upper teeth and trapping air).
- When a /t/ or /d/ is trapped between two other consonants (e.g. *nex**t** **d**oor* or *las**t** **n**ight*), your tongue physically cannot make three separate consonant closures in 100 milliseconds without stumbling.
- English solves this by simply omitting the /t/ or /d/. Native listeners do not even notice it is missing because the brain fills in the gap!
- Spanish speakers, however, are trained from childhood to articulate every single consonant. When Alicia tries to pronounce every /t/ and /d/, her mouth experiences "articulatory gridlock"—she slows down, her jaw stiffens, and her speech becomes robotic.

### Consonant Gemination (The "Twin Glides")
- What happens when a word ends with the same consonant that starts the next word (*bus stop*, *bad dog*, *hot tea*)?
- Beginners try to pronounce both: *bus ... stop* or *hot ... tea*. This creates an ugly, staccato pause.
- Native British speakers **geminate**: they close the mouth once, hold the pressure for a millisecond longer, and release into the next vowel: */bʌsːtɒp/*, */hɒ-tiː/*. It sounds like one elongated word.

### Why Spanish Speakers Say "E-school" (The Epenthetic /e/ Mystery)
Understanding this will make you an extraordinary teacher for Alicia:
- In the history of the Spanish language, Latin words starting with `s + consonant` evolved to require an "e" in front:
  - Latin *schola* ➔ Spanish *escuela*
  - Latin *status* ➔ Spanish *estado*
  - Latin *specialis* ➔ Spanish *especial*
- Because Spanish phonotactic rules forbid any word from starting with `s + consonant`, **Alicia’s brain literally does not possess an automatic motor program for starting a word with /s/ + consonant without a vowel!**
- She doesn't say *"eschool"* or *"estrategy"* because she is careless; her vocal cords are wired to begin vibrating before her tongue reaches the /s/. Your job is to physically rewire that motor circuit.

## 2. Neuro-Linguistic Diagnosis: What is Happening Inside Alicia's Brain?

### The "Over-Enunciation" Anxiety Trap
At C1, Alicia knows she is being evaluated for accuracy. This creates a psychological trap:
- When nervous, she tries to pronounce every single letter clearly: *"I wen-t t-o th-e fir-s-t clas-s projec-t."*
- You must explain to her that **in British English, over-enunciation sounds foreign and unnatural**.
- Native fluency is about knowing which sounds to **sacrifice** to keep the rhythm flowing smoothly.

## 3. Physical Articulation Coaching (Webcam & Mouth Mechanics)

### How to Kill the Epenthetic "e" on Webcam (The "Snake Hiss" Technique)
1. **Explain the Voice Box Vibration**:
   - Have Alicia touch her throat (Adams apple area) with two fingers.
   - Tell her: *"Say 'eh'. Feel that vibration? That is your vocal cords. Now make a snake hissing sound: 'ssss'. Feel that? Zero vibration! It's pure cold air."*
2. **The 1-Second Hiss Exercise**:
   - Tell her: *"Before you say 'strategy', I want you to hiss like a snake for one full second. Do not vibrate your throat until your mouth moves to the 'tr':*  
     `sssssssssssss-trategy`."
   - Do this 5 times. Gradually reduce the hiss from 1 second to 0.2 seconds.
   - This physically disables the vocal cord onset that produces the unwanted "e".

### How to Coach Gemination on Webcam
- Say: *"Watch my mouth on camera for 'hot tea'. Notice that my tongue hits the roof of my mouth for the 't' in 'hot', holds the breath for a split second, and then opens directly into 'tea'. I do NOT say 'hot... tea'. It's one smooth glide: hot‿tea."*

## 4. Word-for-Word Teacher Scripts ("What to Say in Class")

### Script 1: Introducing Consonant Elision (Minute 08:00)
> *"Alicia, today we're going to give your mouth permission to be lazy.  
> In Spanish, every consonant is precious and articulated clearly. In British English, we are ruthlessly efficient. When /t/ or /d/ is surrounded by other consonants, we throw it in the rubbish bin!  
> Listen to me: I don't say 'last... night'; I say 'las-night'. I don't say 'next... door'; I say 'nex-door'.  
> If you say the /t/, you sound like a textbook robot. If you drop it, you sound like a native Londoner. Let's practice!"*

### Script 2: Correcting the "E-strategy" Slip
- **Scenario**: During Phase 3, Alicia says: *"Our company needs an **e-specific** plan."*
- **Your Exact Script**:
  > *"Spot on point, but pause right there! Did you hear that tiny Spanish ghost 'e' sneak into 'specific'?  
  > Put your fingers on your throat. Hiss with me: sssss-pecific. Try it again!"*
- Alicia repeats: *"ssss-pecific."*
- You say: *"Perfect! Clean and sharp. Repeat the whole phrase: 'an s-pecific plan'."*

### Script 3: Recasting "Take Shortcuts" into Native British Idioms
- **Scenario**: In the debate, Alicia says: *"The team took bad shortcuts to finish the project."*
- **Your Script (Immediate Recast)**:
  > *"Right, exactly—they completely **cut corners** just to meet the deadline. And when a team cuts corners like that, does it usually pan out well?"*
  - Type in Preply chat: `cut corners = take shortcuts at the expense of quality`.

## 5. Exercise-by-Exercise Pedagogical Breakdown

### Exercise 2.1: The Clean /s/ Rapid Sprint (Phase 1)
- **Why it's here**: High-repetition motor calibration.
- **Words used**: *strategy, specific, start, skillful, spontaneous, stakeholder*. These are the exact words she will need in her new executive job!
- **Target**: Zero vowel onset. If you hear even a microsecond of "e", make a gentle buzz sound (*"Bzzzt! Try that hiss again!"*) and have her repeat immediately.

### Exercise 2.3: Alveolar Stop Elision Drills (Phase 2)
- **Watch out for**:
  - `nex(t) door` ➔ should sound like `neks-dɔː`.
  - `las(t) night` ➔ should sound like `lɑːs-naɪt`.
  - `hel(d) back` ➔ should sound like `hel-bæk`.
- **The Golden Exception Rule**:
  - Explain to Alicia: *"We only drop /t/ and /d/ before a **consonant**. If the next word begins with a **vowel**, the /t/ or /d/ stays alive because of catenation! Example: 'las(t) week' (drop /t/), but 'last‿of all' (keep /t/ and link it)!"*

### Exercise 2.4: Consonant Gemination (Phase 2)
- Focus on: *Prime Minister* (/praɪ-mɪnɪstə/) and *bus stop* (/bʌ-stɒp/).
- Ensure she doesn't stop her breath between words. It should feel like singing one continuous note.

### Phase 4: High-Level Roleplay Lab (The Product Launch Dilemma)
- **The Psychology of the Scenario**: Alicia is about to start her new job. This simulation tests her ability to stand her ground under pressure from an impatient British manager (Lewis).
- **Your Role as Lewis (The Pushy Director)**:

## 6. Classroom Management, Psychology & Troubleshooting

### Troubleshooting Scenarios ("The Panic Button")
1. **What if Alicia says dropping consonants feels "wrong" or ungrammatical?**
   - *Fix*: Reassure her: *"It feels wrong because you were taught to read English before you were taught the mechanics of native speech. In phonology, this is called coarticulation. The greatest BBC broadcasters drop their /t/ in 'last night'. You are learning real English, not spelling."*
2. **What if the roleplay gets tense or she runs out of arguments?**
   - *Fix*: Step out of character for 5 seconds: *"Great pushback! Now hit me with the financial risk of cutting corners—bring in the phrase 'weigh up'."*
3. **What if she struggles to remember the idioms during the debate?**
   - *Fix*: Point to the screen-shared Student Handout: *"Look at phrase #2 on your handout—use that right now to describe the risk."*
