Exercise 1.2 Deep Dive

The "Anti-Translate" Discourse Starter Clinic

Why this works: Spanish speakers often pause because they are constructing the sentence in Spanish ("La verdad es que...", "Para ser sincera..."). By immediately uttering a British discourse starter, you occupy your vocal cords and force your brain to continue along an English grammatical pathway!

Blunt Spanish-style Thought: Starter: "To be fair..."

"El jefe no nos dio tiempo suficiente, pero él tampoco tenía la información."

Model British Conversion: "To be fair, the manager didn't give us enough time, but to be fair to him, he didn't have all the facts either."
Blunt Spanish-style Thought: Starter: "Quite frankly..."

"Sinceramente, creo que este informe no sirve para nada."

Model British Conversion: "Quite frankly, I think this report is completely unhelpful and misses the core issues."
Blunt Spanish-style Thought: Starter: "It's a bit of a stretch..."

"Decir que tendremos todo listo el viernes es imposible."

Model British Conversion: "It's a bit of a stretch to expect the entire deliverable to be completed by Friday."
Blunt Spanish-style Thought: Starter: "At the end of the day..."

"Al final lo que importa es que el cliente esté contento."

Model British Conversion: "At the end of the day, all that truly matters is whether the client is satisfied with the results."
PART 2

Introducing British Rhythm, Schwa (/ə/) & Catenation

Asynchronous Physical Clinic

1. The Metronome Rule: "Drum Beat" vs. "Machine Gun"

Spanish is a syllable-timed language (da-da-da-da): every syllable takes equal time, and vowels remain pure. British English is a stress-timed language (DUM ... da-da ... DUM): only stressed syllables hit a regular musical beat. All words in between are squashed, blurred, and shortened to make room!

🥁 Try The Physical Desk Tap Drill:

Tap your desk with your knuckle at a steady, rhythmic pace: Tap ... Tap ... Tap. Do NOT slow down your hand! Say each sentence below, fitting all extra words between the beats:

[Tap!] CATS      [Tap!] EAT      [Tap!] FISH. 3 beats / 3 syllables
[Tap!] The CATS    [Tap!] will EAT    [Tap!] the FISH. 3 beats / 5 syllables (same time!)
[Tap!] The CATS might have    [Tap!] EAT-en all the    [Tap!] FISH. 3 beats / 8 syllables (same time!)

Notice how "might have" and "all the" had to be squashed into tiny whispered sounds? That is English rhythm!

2. How to Make the Schwa (/ə/) in Your Mouth

The Schwa is not an exotic sound. It is the sound your mouth makes when it does zero work.

The "Lazy Sigh" Technique

  1. Relax your shoulders and let your jaw hang half-open.
  2. Don't round your lips (no "o"), don't smile (no "e").
  3. Let your tongue lie completely limp in the bottom of your mouth.
  4. Make a quiet, lazy grunt: /uh/ ➔ /ə/.

Spanish Trap vs. British Reality

  • community: Don't round lips into "co-". Say /kəˈmjuː.nə.ti/.
  • suppose: Don't say "soo-pose". Say /səˈpəʊz/.
  • woman: Second syllable is lazy: /ˈwʊm.ən/.
  • Hampshire: Don't say "-shire". Say /ˈhæmp.ʃə/.

3. Consonant-to-Vowel Catenation (Ligature ‿)

In British speech, words do not stop abruptly. If a word ends in a consonant and the next starts with a vowel, the consonant leaps across like a bridge:

hold + on
hol-don
turned + out
turn-dout
pick + it + up
pi-ki-tup
first + of + all
firs-tə-vɔːl
Independent Practice

Schwa Reduction & Catenation Marking

Read these 5 sentences out loud. Type your notes or transcription in the input, then reveal the model annotation:

1. It turned out to be an unexpected gathering of community leaders.

Marked: It turned ‿ out to be an ‿ unexpected gathering ‿ of community leaders.

Catenation: /tɜː-naʊt/, /ə-nʌn-/, /ˈɡæð.ər.ɪ-ŋəv/. Schwa in community: /kəˈmjuː.nə.ti/.

2. Hold on a second, to be fair he made an enormous effort.

Marked: Hold ‿ on ‿ a second, to be fair he made ‿ an ‿ enormous ‿ effort.

Glide: /həʊl-dɒ-nə/, /meɪ-də-nɪ-nɔː-mə-sefət/. Effort in RP: /ˈef.ət/.

3. At the end of the day, an official announcement was made in Hampshire.

Marked: At the end ‿ of the day, an ‿ official ‿ announcement was made ‿ in Hampshire.

Hampshire is /ˈhæmp.ʃə/. Linked: /en-dəv/, /meɪ-dɪn/.

4. Quite frankly, it is a bit of a stretch to suppose she will brush it off.

Marked: Quite frankly, it ‿ is ‿ a bit ‿ of ‿ a stretch to suppose she will brush ‿ it ‿ off.

Linked: /ɪ-tɪ-zə/, /bɪ-tə-və/, /brʌ-ʃɪ-tɒf/. Suppose: /səˈpəʊz/.

5. Can I pick it up from the woman at the front desk?

Marked: Can ‿ I pick ‿ it ‿ up from the woman at the front desk?

Continuous chain: /pɪ-kɪ-tʌp/. Weak modal: /kən/. Woman: /ˈwʊm.ən/.

PART 3

Extending the Lexicon: Beyond the First 4 Starters

Phrasal Verbs & Perceptions

In class, we mastered the 4 discourse starters (To be fair, Quite frankly, A bit of a stretch, At the end of the day). Now, add these 3 essential British expressions to complete your Week 1 toolkit:

Come across (as)
How you are perceived by others.
"In the client presentation, she came across as exceptionally poised."
🚫 Spanish error: "give the impression of"
Turn out
To result in or end up as.
"We expected a dull conference, but it turned out to be brilliant."
🚫 Spanish error: "result in" / "end up"
Brush off
To dismiss criticism or setbacks lightly.
"He just brushed off their rude comments and carried on."
🚫 Spanish error: "pass from" / "ignore"
